Every three years, the OECD publishes a global education report card — PISA. In 2025, over 760,000 fifteen-year-olds across 91 countries were tested in science, mathematics, reading and, for the first time, computational problem solving. This guide explains what PISA is, how scores are calculated, what the 2025 results reveal globally and for Turkey — and, most importantly, why PISA skills are among the strongest predictors of career success in the modern economy.

What Is PISA? The 2025 Global Results, Score Calculation and What It Means for Your Child's Future

What is PISA PISA 2025 results PISA score explained PISA career impact

I've been working in international education for over twenty years. And every three years, when the OECD publishes the PISA results, I experience the same quiet unease — not because the numbers are bad, but because most families never see them. They look at their child's school report, see good grades, and assume that's the full picture. It rarely is.

PISA tells a different story. It asks not "what has your child memorised?" but "what can your child actually do with what they know?" And in a world that increasingly values the ability to solve novel problems over the ability to recall stored answers, that distinction matters enormously.

What Is PISA?

PISA stands for Programme for International Student Assessment. It is run by the OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) every three years, and it is the most comprehensive international comparison of educational outcomes in existence.

Three facts distinguish it from other assessments:

  • Age-based, not grade-based. Any 15-year-old in a participating country can be selected, regardless of what year of school they are in.
  • Genuinely global scale. In 2025, 91 countries and economies participated. Over 760,000 students were tested, representing approximately 33 million 15-year-olds worldwide.
  • Applied skills, not curriculum knowledge. PISA does not test what students have been taught — it tests whether they can apply reasoning to real-world problems they have never seen before.

The three core domains are mathematics, science and reading comprehension. In 2025, a fourth domain was assessed for the first time: computational problem solving — not coding, but the capacity to think algorithmically, interpret data and solve problems in digital environments. This is the skill at the centre of the AI economy.

PISA measures education systems, not individual students. No child receives a personal PISA score. The results describe what a country's 15-year-olds can do on average — and that tells you a great deal about the system your child is growing up inside.

How Are PISA Scores Calculated?

The test itself

Each student completes a two-hour test. Crucially, not everyone sees the same questions — different students receive different combinations of subject areas (science + reading, mathematics + science, etc.). This design allows PISA to cover far more ground than any individual student could answer while keeping test time manageable.

After the test, students complete a 35-minute background questionnaire covering family circumstances, learning habits, digital device use, attitudes to school and wellbeing. This contextual data is what allows PISA to separate system effects from socio-economic effects.

Item Response Theory (IRT)

PISA scores are not based on raw correct/incorrect counts. They use Item Response Theory (IRT) — a statistical framework that accounts for each question's difficulty and discriminating power, as well as the pattern of each student's responses. The result is a latent ability estimate rather than a simple sum.

All scores are then placed on a common scale normalised so that the OECD average sits near 500 points. This means a Turkish student's score can be directly compared with a Japanese, Finnish or Singaporean student's score.

PISA 2025: What the Global Results Show

The PISA 2025 results, published 8 September 2026, reveal a picture that should give pause to education systems and families worldwide.

Overall: performance stagnating or declining across most OECD countries

Across OECD countries, average scores in science fell modestly between 2015 and 2025. Reading saw particularly large drops in many systems. Mathematics broadly held. The OECD's own characterisation: "largely stagnating or continuing to decline."

This is the global backdrop. Against it, countries that improved stand out sharply.

New in 2025: computational problem solving and AI

The 2025 cycle introduced the first ever assessment of computational problem solving — the ability to use algorithmic thinking, conduct experiments and build solutions in digital environments. This is not coding; it is the cognitive infrastructure that makes AI tools useful rather than passive.

PISA also collected unprecedented data on AI use in education. The finding that has generated the most discussion: students who used AI chatbots for specific schoolwork tasks (summarising, drafting, researching) scored lower in science than students who did not. Passive AI use appears to displace rather than augment thinking.

The misinformation finding: PISA 2025 found that over half of students in OECD countries may be vulnerable to misinformation and disinformation — lacking the critical reading and reasoning skills to distinguish reliable from unreliable sources. This is not a technology problem. It is a literacy problem.

Turkey is one of the few countries to have improved in all three core subjects between 2022 and 2025. Against a backdrop of widespread global decline, this is a genuine achievement.

Why PISA Skills Predict Career Success

This is the question that gets asked least and matters most: does any of this translate into real life?

The answer, backed by OECD longitudinal research across dozens of countries, is yes — strongly. The skills PISA measures correlate with adult earnings, employment quality, health outcomes and civic participation. Not weakly, not ambiguously: the relationship is robust and consistent across systems.

Here is why, concretely:

Reading comprehension → every knowledge-economy role

Parsing a contract, interpreting a research report, writing instructions a team can follow, identifying the flaw in a proposal — these all require the same underlying skill PISA measures in reading. A student who cannot reach Level 2 in reading will struggle with every white-collar role that exists. A student at Level 5–6 will learn new roles faster than their peers, every time.

Quantitative reasoning → decision-making under uncertainty

Managing a project budget, evaluating risk, reading a P&L, interpreting clinical data, understanding interest rates — none of these require advanced mathematics. All of them require the numerical reasoning PISA measures at Levels 2–4. This is not a skill for mathematicians; it is a skill for anyone who will ever need to make a consequential decision.

Computational problem solving → the AI economy

The ability to think algorithmically — to decompose a problem, design a process, test a solution — is what separates someone who uses AI tools productively from someone who is replaced by them. PISA measured this for the first time in 2025. Countries that score well here are building workforces suited to the next fifty years of the economy. Countries that score poorly are not.

What Should Families Do?

1. Stop measuring your child against their class

Being top of a Turkish school and being globally competitive are two different things. PISA makes this explicit. Measure your child against an international standard — early, while there is time to act on the result.

2. Prioritise applied skills over content knowledge

Reading comprehension, mathematical reasoning, algorithmic thinking — PISA measures these because employers value them. Tuition that builds these skills is worth far more than tuition that builds exam scores.

3. Take computational skills seriously

Turkey scored below the OECD average in computational problem solving — the one domain most directly linked to AI-era career readiness. This gap can be closed, but only if it is first acknowledged.

4. Use AI as a thinking partner, not a shortcut

PISA 2025 found that students who used AI to do tasks — rather than to think through tasks — scored lower. The goal is not to ban AI; it is to use it in ways that build rather than bypass cognition.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is PISA in simple terms?

PISA is the world's largest international education assessment. Every three years, the OECD tests 15-year-olds across 91 countries in mathematics, science and reading. In 2025, computational problem solving was added. PISA measures applied skills — not what students have memorised, but what they can actually do with what they know.

What were the PISA 2025 results?

Results were published 8 September 2026. Top performers globally: China (B-S-J-Z), Singapore, Japan, Korea, Estonia and the UK. Most OECD countries saw stagnating or declining scores, particularly in reading. Turkey was among a small number of countries that improved in all three core subjects compared to 2022.

How are PISA scores calculated?

PISA uses Item Response Theory (IRT) — a statistical model that accounts for question difficulty and each student's response pattern. Scores are normalised to an OECD average of approximately 500 points. Results are grouped into 7 proficiency levels (1b to 6); Level 2 is the baseline competency threshold and Level 5–6 represents globally competitive performance.

What is a good PISA score?

The OECD average is approximately 500 points. Level 2 is the minimum considered adequate for modern life (around 407–480 points depending on the domain). Level 5–6 (above 607 points) represents globally competitive students. Singapore consistently places 37% of students at Level 5–6 in mathematics; Turkey places 8% — equal to the OECD average.

Does PISA predict career success?

Yes. OECD longitudinal research consistently shows that the skills PISA measures — reading comprehension, quantitative reasoning and problem solving — correlate strongly with adult earnings and employment quality across all participating countries. These are not test-taking skills; they are the cognitive foundations of knowledge-economy work.

PISA Puanı Nasıl Hesaplanır?

Her öğrenci 2 saatlik bir test alır — ancak herkes aynı soruları görmez. PISA, Madde Tepki Teorisi (IRT) adlı istatistiksel yöntemi kullanır: her sorunun güçlük düzeyi ve öğrencinin yanıt örüntüsü hesaba katılarak uluslararası ölçeğe hizalanmış bir yetkinlik tahmini üretilir. OECD ortalaması ~500 puan olacak şekilde normalize edilir. Sonuçlar Level 1b'den Level 6'ya kadar 7 yetkinlik düzeyine ayrılır; Level 2 temel eşik, Level 5–6 ise küresel rekabetçi performansı temsil eder.

PISA 2025 Küresel Sonuçlar

8 Eylül 2026'da yayımlanan sonuçlar: Çin (B-S-J-Z), Singapur, Japonya, Güney Kore, Estonya ve İngiltere küresel liderler arasında. OECD ülkelerinde genel performans düşüyor — özellikle okumada büyük gerilemeler var. Türkiye ise 2022'ye kıyasla üç alanda birden ilerleme kaydeden nadir ülkelerden biri.

2025'in en önemli yeniliği: hesaplamalı problem çözme testi. Yapay zeka çağının merkezindeki bu beceri alanında Türkiye OECD ortalamasının altında kaldı. Ayrıca PISA, yapay zeka kullanan öğrencilerin fen puanlarının AI kullanmayanlara göre daha düşük olduğunu ortaya koydu — pasif AI kullanımı düşünmeyi geliştirmiyor, yerinden ediyor.

PISA Becerileri Neden İş Başarısını Öngörüyor?

OECD'nin onlarca ülkede yürüttüğü boylamsal araştırmalar şunu gösteriyor: PISA'nın ölçtüğü beceriler — okuma anlama, sayısal muhakeme, problem çözme — yetişkinlik döneminde gelir düzeyi ve istihdam kalitesiyle güçlü bir ilişki içinde. Bu "sınav becerileri" değil; bilgi ekonomisinde işlev görebilmenin bilişsel temelleri.
