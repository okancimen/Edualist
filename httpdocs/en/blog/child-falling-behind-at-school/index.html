My Child Is Falling Behind at School: Find the Cause First

The report card arrives. You hold it and look at the pages. Last term was better. Or maybe last year. Something changed — but when? How? Teachers say "needs to work harder." Your child says "I don't know." And you're stuck between those two sentences.

Academic decline is one of the most suffocating worries a parent can face — because there is a visible problem and an unclear path forward. Tutoring? Academic coach? Change schools? See a specialist? Try all of them?

In this article I want to explain why there is no need to rush — but also why there is every reason not to pause. Because academic decline does not have a single cause, and the right intervention begins with finding the right one.

The 4 Root Causes of Academic Decline

Working with families over many years, I have found that academic decline almost always traces back to one of four root causes — or a combination:

  1. Level mismatch: Working below the class level — or, less discussed but equally important, working far above it.
  2. Motivational and emotional factors: Anxiety, social tensions, major transitions, family stress.
  3. Learning-style mismatch: A gap between how the child learns and how the school teaches.
  4. An underlying cognitive difficulty: Dyslexia, ADHD, processing differences — conditions that affect performance but have not been diagnosed.

From the outside, all four look the same: the student isn't studying, homework isn't finished, grades are falling. But each requires a completely different response. And the wrong response — particularly tutoring pitched at the wrong level — can deepen the problem rather than fix it.

1. Level Mismatch: Behind the Class — or Well Ahead of It

The most familiar scenario: the child isn't grasping the material and is working below class level. This is visible and the response is relatively clear.

But there is a less-discussed scenario that matters just as much: highly capable or fast-learning children who are significantly ahead of the class. These children show boredom, disengagement, and sometimes disruptive behaviour in lessons. Their grades may drop — not because they can't do the work, but because they have lost the motivation to do it on demand. From the outside, this looks identical to a struggling student.

Standard grades conceal both scenarios. A student scoring 65 might be far below the class level, or might be bored and disengaged. Distinguishing between them requires looking past report marks.

An EduEntry academic assessment reveals your child's true academic position — independently of school grades. Which topics have genuine gaps, and where does unexpected strength appear? Seeing this is the fastest way to diagnose a level mismatch.

Grades tell you how good or bad things are. An academic assessment tells you why.

2. Motivational and Emotional Factors

Academic decline is not always an academic problem. Sometimes something happening in the child's life — at school or outside it — surfaces in academic performance.

Common triggers: changing schools, the start of adolescence, tension or exclusion within friendship groups, parental separation, a bereavement, or persistent low-level anxiety.

What these factors share is this: they all look like academic failure. Homework isn't done, focus is absent in class, grades fall. But the underlying cause is emotional, not academic. Starting tutoring in this situation doesn't help — in fact it can feel like extra weight and increase anxiety.

The first step is simple but most parents skip it: have a non-academic conversation with your child. "What changed at school this term? How are things with your friends?" The answers often say a great deal.

Where emotional factors are prominent — persistent anxiety, school refusal, social withdrawal — school counselling or a child psychologist should be engaged before any academic intervention.

3. Learning-Style Mismatch

Children learn in genuinely different ways. Some grasp things fastest through visual presentation; others through listening, or doing. Some are most productive in the morning; others in the evening. Some need frequent short repetitions; others need long, concentrated blocks.

A traditional classroom — especially large groups with a single delivery style — reaches only a small range of these differences. When a child's learning style does not match the school's teaching style, the child is working but getting no traction. From the outside this looks like laziness or disinterest.

Signs of this scenario: the child does significantly better with certain teachers or certain ways of presenting material; understands when something is explained orally but loses the thread when it is written down; grasps things at home but cannot apply them in class.

The solution is to understand the child's learning style and adapt the study plan accordingly. This is the application of differentiated learning principles at home — and it requires recognising that style first.

4. Cognitive Difficulties: When to Seek a Specialist

The fourth category is the one parents are most reluctant to consider, but also the most important to discuss openly: undiagnosed cognitive difficulties.

Dyslexia affects reading and writing speed and comprehension; it shows up in exams and assignments but has nothing to do with intelligence. ADHD affects attention and impulse control; it becomes particularly visible in long-term projects and tasks that require sitting still. Processing differences are a less-discussed but common category: differences in the speed and method by which information is processed.

None of these prevent academic success — with the right support and adjustments. But working without a diagnosis, with the wrong methods and without appropriate support, is both inefficient and erodes the student's confidence.

Red flags: decline is continuing across multiple subjects and has been going on for some time; reading and writing speed is significantly below the class average; the child understands when doing homework but can't apply it in an exam; serious exhaustion and frustration appear quickly during study.

If you see these signs, request an assessment from a school psychologist or a child development specialist — not to label, but to understand what is actually happening.

The First 4 Weeks After You Notice a Decline

When you first notice academic decline, a structured process works far better than panic. Here is a recommended four-week sequence:

  • Week 1 — Observe, don't intervene: Watch homework time. Which subjects cause sticking points? When does energy dip? Are there particular types of task the child avoids? Take notes — don't guess.
  • Week 2 — Measure the real academic level: Use an EduEntry assessment or equivalent to see your child's actual position objectively. School grades are not enough.
  • Week 3 — Talk to the teacher: Instead of "the grades are slipping," ask: "How has the child been in class over the last month?" Teachers usually notice before parents do — but they may not raise it unprompted.
  • Week 4 — Decision point: You now have observation, assessment data, and teacher feedback. Choose one of three paths: academic coaching (for management and method support); subject tutoring (if there is a genuine content gap in a specific subject); or specialist referral (if cognitive or emotional signals are clear).

Is Tutoring Enough?

Tutoring is the right solution in the right situation. When the problem is a specific knowledge gap — trigonometry, essay structure — additional explanation and practice helps.

But tutoring doesn't work — and can make things worse — in these situations:

  • If the problem is the study system rather than understanding: tutoring adds more material without building the system.
  • If the problem is motivation: extra study hours don't raise motivation; they usually exhaust it.
  • If tutoring is pitched at the wrong level: grade-level explanations miss a child whose real level is lower.

Academic coaching, unlike tutoring, does not deliver content. Instead it supports the student in building their own study system, time management, and learning strategy. If the root cause of falling behind is management or method — coaching is far more effective than tutoring.

Combining both is also possible: coaching builds the system, tutoring fills the specific content gap. But the order matters — build the system first, then fill the content.

What should I do if my child is falling behind academically?

The first step is to find the cause. Academic decline has four possible roots: level mismatch, motivational or emotional factors, learning-style mismatch, or an undiagnosed cognitive difficulty. Rather than immediately starting tutoring, the right starting point is to measure the child's real academic level objectively (with a tool like EduEntry) and speak with the teacher.

When grades drop, should I get a tutor or an academic coach?

Tutoring works when the problem is a specific knowledge gap. But if the issue is study habits, project management, motivation, or a learning-style mismatch, tutoring only adds to the child's load without fixing anything. Academic coaching addresses root causes and helps the student build their own working system.

When should I seek professional help?

Several red flags: the decline has continued for more than 3 weeks; it is happening across multiple subjects simultaneously; school refusal or intense anxiety accompanies it; or the teacher has reported an unusual change in behaviour. A school counsellor or child psychologist is the right first step.
