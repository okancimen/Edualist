My Child Can't Learn English: Method, Level or Motivation?

Child can't learn English English not progressing despite tutoring International school English preparation BICS vs CALP

Private tutoring. Regular sessions. A good teacher. Three months in — still no progress. The vocabulary isn't sticking. Full sentences aren't coming. In class, questions get answered with a frozen silence. And you're asking yourself: is there something wrong with my child, or is it the method?

That is exactly the right question. And the answer is almost always the same: the problem isn't the child — it's the starting point.

There is no single reason why a child can't learn English. After years of working with families moving from Turkey to Dubai and other international school systems, I've found four recurring categories behind "my child isn't improving." Each one requires a completely different solution.

The 4 Real Reasons Children Can't Learn English

Reason 1: Method Mismatch

This is the most common cause and the least discussed. Private tutoring and language courses typically work in a single format: grammar explanation, exercises, vocabulary memorisation. This works well for some learners — but if the learning style doesn't match, nothing sticks.

A visual learner given purely text-based content. An active, hands-on learner asked to sit and listen. The mismatch shows up in a specific pattern: the child understands during the lesson but forgets everything within a week. They say "yes, got it" in the session but can't use the language in a real situation. This is not a knowledge problem — it's a format problem.

The fix is to understand the learning style first, then change the format. Academic support for expat children that uses differentiated instruction is especially critical in English learning, because language acquisition depends on live context — not abstract rules memorised in isolation.

Reason 2: Level Mismatch

The second common cause is a mismatch between your child's real English level and what the class or tutor is teaching. This can go in either direction.

If a child is at A1 but working with B1 material — which is very common, since most programmes group by age rather than by actual ability — there is no foundation. Every new topic arrives before the previous one was understood. No matter how hard they work, nothing accumulates.

The reverse is equally damaging: a child who has solid A2 placed in an A1 group quickly becomes bored, disengages, and the resulting drop in effort gets misread as "lazy" or "unmotivated" when they are simply under-stimulated.

The only reliable way to identify a level mismatch is an objective assessment. The level a programme assigns by default cannot be trusted — groups are rarely as homogeneous as they appear.

Reason 3: Motivation Gap

The third cause is more philosophical: the child has no answer to "why am I learning this?" In many educational systems, English is positioned as a school subject — something you study for exams, not something you use. This makes the language feel like a burden rather than a tool.

The signal is specific: the child performs well in every other subject but shows particular disengagement in English. Or they freely use English in games, YouTube, social media — but shut down in a formal lesson environment. This child can, but won't. The issue is not ability.

The fix is to make the language real. "If you get good at English, you can study in Dubai." "You can join the forum for your favourite game." "That YouTube video you watch every day is in English — are you catching what they're saying?" Connecting the abstract to something genuinely relevant converts motivation from an obligation into a reason.

Reason 4: Vocabulary Threshold

The fourth cause is technical but critically important. Language research consistently shows that you cannot learn from a text or conversation unless you already understand at least 80% of it. Below that threshold, there is not enough context for new words or patterns to register. The wheel spins but nothing moves forward.

If your child knows 200–300 words and the average school text contains 1,000 different words, they cannot understand it. Because they cannot understand it, they cannot learn from it. Because they don't feel they're learning, they conclude "English is pointless." This is a cycle — and the only exit is to build core vocabulary to a critical mass before expecting reading or listening input to stick.

There are 4 distinct reasons a child can't learn English — and each requires a different fix. Starting private tutoring without identifying the cause first is like prescribing medicine without asking what's wrong.

Why Private Tutoring Doesn't Always Work

Private tutoring, in the right conditions, is highly effective. But the reflex of "my child isn't improving in English, let's get a private tutor" fails in many situations — sometimes becoming a pure waste of time and money. Here's why.

First problem: most private tutors start without an objective level test. They don't assess the child's real level, identify specific gaps, or analyse their learning style. They simply begin with a standard curriculum — prescribing without diagnosing.

Second problem: private tutoring is 1–2 hours per week. Language acquisition requires intensive exposure — not just lesson time, but daily contact with the language. Tutoring alone cannot provide this. Without a daily language environment, tutoring produces limited returns.

Third problem: if the level is wrong, the best tutor in the world can't compensate. You cannot add floors to a building with no foundation.

When these three problems are addressed — right level, right method, right amount of exposure — private tutoring becomes very effective. But that requires an objective assessment first.

English for International School: A Different Calculation

If your child is about to start at an international school in Dubai or elsewhere, the question "my child can't learn English" takes on a different urgency. Because in an international school, English is not just a subject — it is the medium of learning.

A child with limited English in a Dubai international school faces two simultaneous challenges: they cannot communicate socially, and they cannot follow the academic content of every other subject — maths, science, humanities, all taught in English. EAL (English as Additional Language) programmes exist to bridge this gap, but relying on EAL means your child progresses at a slower pace than peers in core subjects, often for years.

This is why building English as far as possible before school starts is worth the investment. And it's why understanding how a limited-English child adapts to a Dubai school matters for setting realistic expectations.

International school English preparation has two distinct dimensions:

  • Social English (BICS — Basic Interpersonal Communication Skills): Chatting with peers, understanding instructions, navigating social situations. This typically develops within 6–12 months of immersion.
  • Academic English (CALP — Cognitive Academic Language Proficiency): Following lesson content, writing essays, processing abstract concepts in English. This takes 3–7 years to reach full academic proficiency.

This distinction matters enormously. After 2–3 months at school, your child may appear fluent in conversation — BICS has developed. But if CALP is still weak, subject grades will reflect it. Many families mistake the social fluency signal for readiness and are surprised when academic results don't follow.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I help my child learn English when nothing seems to work?

Start by identifying which of the four root causes applies: method mismatch, level mismatch, motivation gap, or vocabulary threshold. Each requires a different solution. Method mismatch means trying different formats — visual, hands-on, audio — rather than repeating the same approach. Level mismatch means getting an objective assessment and placing your child at their real starting point. Motivation gap means connecting English to something your child genuinely cares about. Vocabulary threshold means building core vocabulary to 1,000+ words before expecting reading or listening to stick. Starting tutoring without diagnosing the cause first usually wastes time and money.

How long does it take a child to learn English at different ages?

Age matters significantly in second language acquisition. Ages 0–7 is the fastest and most natural period — with enough exposure, children acquire language almost instinctively. Ages 8–12 is still highly effective; with structured learning, a solid foundation can be built in 2–3 years. After 13, acquisition requires more conscious effort, though there is no real ceiling — just a different method. For international school readiness, basic conversational English (BICS) typically takes 6–12 months; academic English (CALP) can take 3–7 years to reach full proficiency.

What English level is needed for a Dubai international school?

Most Dubai international schools teach entirely in English, but few publish a formal minimum. In practice, CEFR A2–B1 (basic communication plus ability to follow classroom instructions) is expected at entry. Students with weaker English are placed in EAL (English as Additional Language) programmes, which vary significantly between schools. EAL support helps, but it also means your child progresses at a different pace from peers in core academic subjects. A pre-admission English assessment gives you an objective picture of where your child stands and how much preparation is realistic.
