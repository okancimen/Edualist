Tutoring vs Academic Coaching: Knowing the Difference Saves Hundreds of Hours

Tutoring vs academic coaching Academic coach vs tutor When to use academic coaching Tutor or coach for my child

For two years I paid for tutoring twice a week. The tutor was good — patient, clear, and easy-going with my child. After each session the homework was done. When exams arrived, the grades stayed exactly where they'd been before.

At the end of year one I asked myself: "Why isn't anything changing?" At the end of year two I asked a different question: "Am I paying for the wrong thing?"

The answer was yes. I was paying for the wrong thing. My child's problem wasn't a knowledge gap. The problem was study habits, time management, and an inability to apply what was understood. Tutoring fixes none of those things. I learnt that in week six of academic coaching — not in the two years of tutoring that preceded it.

If you are about to ask that same question, this article is for you.

What Does Tutoring Do — and What Can't It Do?

Tutoring does one thing well: it closes knowledge gaps. It re-explains a topic that was taught poorly or not understood. It revisits a concept missed in class. It gives targeted support for a specific piece of homework or an upcoming test.

That is an extremely valuable function — in the right situation. If a child has moved from one school to another and there are topics missing from the new curriculum, tutoring bridges those gaps. If important content was missed due to illness or travel, tutoring works well. If a teacher's explanation simply doesn't land for a particular child, a tutor offers a different angle.

But there are situations where tutoring does not work, and knowing them is important.

If the child understands the material but cannot apply it in an exam, tutoring doesn't help. If the child grasps the homework but can't bring themselves to start it, tutoring doesn't help. If the child cannot plan multi-week assignments in IB or A-Level, tutoring doesn't help. If the problem is motivation, tutoring doesn't help.

The most dangerous trap tutoring creates is dependency. With the tutor present, everything gets done. When the tutor leaves, the child freezes. Sitting at the desk, nothing happens — because the habit formed is not learning, but waiting to be taught. Once that loop is established, it becomes hard to stop tutoring at all: take it away and nothing seems to happen, so it feels safer to keep it.

Tutoring closes knowledge gaps. Academic coaching closes skill and system gaps. Confusing the two makes both ineffective.

What Does an Academic Coach Do — and What Can't They Do?

Academic coaching asks a completely different question: "Why can't this child work independently?" and "What does the child need in order to?"

An academic coach does not teach subject content. In a coaching session you won't find explanations of quadratic equations or the causes of World War One. Instead a session looks like this: how much of last week's plan actually happened, what felt hard, what got in the way, what system could we build to get past that obstacle? Goals are set, concrete steps are planned, and the next session starts with accountability.

Coaching produces strong results in these situations: the child understands the material but isn't working, or is procrastinating; the student is struggling to submit work on time in an intensive IB or A-Level programme; there is a motivation problem and no sustainable working rhythm; executive function skills (planning, organisation, prioritisation) are underdeveloped; the student has transferred to an international school and cannot adapt to the new system's expectations.

But there are things coaching cannot fix either. If a child genuinely has never learnt the content — if fractions were never covered, if basic chemistry concepts are missing — coaching won't help there. A coach does not teach content. That content gap needs to be closed first. This is why, in some situations, both supports are needed together — but more on that below.

What does a coaching session actually look like? It does not begin with "let me explain this topic to you." The coach asks: "Why didn't you do what you planned last week? What happened in that time? If the same situation comes up next week, what will you do instead?" These questions turn the child into an observer of their own behaviour — and that observation gradually becomes self-regulation.

Outcome Short-term grade improvement Long-term independence

Looking at that table, the two supports are not rivals — they answer different questions. The issue is never which one is "better," but which problem each one solves.

Which One Does My Child Need? 5 Questions

Before deciding, ask yourself these five questions. Each answer points you in the right direction.

1. Does the child know the material but not perform in exams?
At home they answer questions correctly; in the exam the results don't come. This is not a content problem — it is an application and stress-management problem. Answer: Coach.

2. Is the topic genuinely not understood?
When you try to explain, the child says "I don't know" and genuinely doesn't. Core concepts are missing. This is a content gap. Answer: Tutor.

3. Is the child completing homework but not improving test scores?
Sitting at the desk, work gets done, but exam performance isn't shifting. There may be surface-level studying or a gap between doing and understanding. Answer: Coach.

4. Does the child understand with a tutor but can't work alone?
This can be both a content issue and an independent-study issue. Some core concepts may not be consolidated, and at the same time there is no study system at home. Answer: Both — but in separate roles. Tutor for content, coach for systems.

5. Is there no study system at all?
The child doesn't know when exams are, doesn't track deadlines, doesn't know where to start. This is an executive function problem. Answer: Start with a coach.

Why Tutoring Works Less Well in IB, A-Level, and International Schools

I want to answer this clearly, because it is the most common mistake I see at Edualist.

IB and A-Level assessment, as IBO.org makes clear, does not test content knowledge alone — or at least not content knowledge in isolation. The IB Extended Essay is a three-thousand-word original research piece. What is assessed is depth of thought, source use, argument construction, and time management. Those skills are not developed through weekly tutoring sessions.

A-Level coursework is a process that unfolds over weeks: the student must plan each next step, receive feedback, and revise. That requires planning and self-management skills — coaching, not tutoring.

Compare this with exam-based systems like the Turkish national university exam (YKS), which are content-focused and test-technique-driven. Tutoring was designed for that model and works well within it. International programmes are skill-focused. Tutoring there is the wrong tool — like trying to hammer a nail with a screwdriver.

This does not mean a student entering an international school or starting IB should never use tutoring — if there is a genuine content gap, tutoring fills it. But to manage the system, plan the programme, and meet submission deadlines, academic coaching is what is needed.

Can You Use Both Together?

Yes — but only if the roles are kept clearly separate.

The most functional combination looks like this: a maths tutor closes content gaps; an academic coach builds the study system and time-management skills. Neither duplicates the other's work. The tutor does not try to coach; the coach does not try to teach.

In practice, however, this separation blurs constantly. The tutor gradually starts having "motivational conversations." The coach gradually says "let me just explain this quickly." And you end up paying for both while both are doing the same thing.

To prevent this, whenever you engage either support, ask one question: "What is this person's exact job?" Any session where the answer is unclear is probably wasting time and money.

Using both together works — but only if the roles don't overlap.

If your tutor is having motivational conversations and your coach is explaining content, both are in the wrong place.

A Practical Decision Tree: What to Do When

When you are unsure, follow these steps.

First, observe. What does your child understand in class, and what do they do at home? Is homework being completed — and where does it stall? How are they preparing for exams, or not preparing?

Then measure the real academic level. A school report grade does not reflect true level. Some children work above capacity just to stay at the class average — that is exhausting. Others appear below their real level because the material doesn't interest them. An EduEntry academic assessment measures genuine understanding and distinguishes which gaps are content gaps and which are system problems.

Then decide. If the content gap is large, start with tutoring. If system and motivation problems dominate, start with coaching. If both are present, starting with coaching first is usually more effective — because without a working system, tutoring becomes inefficient anyway.

A decision made without an assessment is a guess.

And guesses cost years. An assessment gives you results in weeks.

Tutoring vs academic coaching — which is better?

They solve different problems. Tutoring fills knowledge gaps; academic coaching addresses study systems, motivation, and time management. Without the right diagnosis, either can be a waste of money.

When should academic coaching start?

Before the problem becomes entrenched — ideally when starting a new programme or school. A student switching to IB or transferring to an international school benefits most from coaching in the first month, before a crisis develops.

Is tutoring pointless?

No. Tutoring is extremely valuable for genuine content gaps. It becomes ineffective when it is used to solve a skill or system problem — one that content delivery cannot fix.
