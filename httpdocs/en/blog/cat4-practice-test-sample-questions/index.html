CAT4 Practice Test 2026: Sample Questions for Every Battery

CAT4 practice test CAT4 sample questions CAT4 preparation Dubai CAT4 verbal reasoning CAT4 non-verbal reasoning
In this guide: Sample question formats for all four CAT4 batteries — Verbal Reasoning, Quantitative Reasoning, Non-Verbal Reasoning and Spatial Ability — plus what scores Dubai KHDA Outstanding schools expect for Year 7 entry and how to prepare effectively.

The CAT4 is the most widely used admissions test at Dubai's British-curriculum secondary schools. Most Year 7 applicants at JESS, Kings' School, Brighton College, Dubai British School Emirates Hills and Jumeirah College sit some form of the CAT4 as part of their application. Yet many families arrive at assessment day without their child having seen a single example question.

This guide provides example question types for each battery so children know what to expect — not to pre-teach answers, but to remove the surprise factor on test day. For a full explanation of how the CAT4 works, see our CAT4 overview guide.

The CAT4 is administered at Level D for most Year 6–7 applicants. It is computer-based at most Dubai schools. Each battery is strictly timed — students are not expected to complete every question.

Battery 1: Verbal Reasoning — Sample Question Types

Verbal reasoning tests the ability to think logically about language — not vocabulary per se, but the ability to spot relationships, analogies and patterns in words.

Type 1: Verbal Analogies

The question presents a word pair and asks you to complete a parallel pair.

Example: Hot is to Cold as Day is to ___

Options: A) Sun   B) Night   C) Bright   D) Time

Answer: B — Night (opposite relationship)

Example: Architect is to Building as Composer is to ___

Options: A) Music   B) Concert   C) Piano   D) Conductor

Answer: A — Music (creator to creation)

Type 2: Odd One Out

Three or four words share a category; one does not belong.

Example: Which word is the odd one out?
Robin   Sparrow   Salmon   Eagle

Answer: Salmon — the only one that is not a bird

Example: Oak   Pine   Birch   Rose

Answer: Rose — the only one that is not a tree

Type 3: Sentence Completion

A sentence with a missing word; choose the word that makes the most logical sense.

Example: The detective studied the clues ___ before reaching a conclusion.

Options: A) rapidly   B) carelessly   C) methodically   D) secretly

Answer: C — methodically (studying clues = systematic/careful approach)

Battery 2: Quantitative Reasoning — Sample Question Types

Quantitative reasoning tests the ability to spot numerical relationships and patterns. This is not arithmetic — it requires logical thinking about numbers, not calculation speed.

Type 1: Number Sequences

Example: What comes next?   2, 5, 10, 17, 26, ___

Answer: 37   (differences: +3, +5, +7, +9, +11 — odd numbers increasing)

Example: 2, 6, 18, 54, ___

Answer: 162   (multiply by 3 each time)

Type 2: Quantitative Analogies

Example: 6 is to 36 as 5 is to ___

Answer: 25   (each number is squared)

Example: 4 is to 12 as 7 is to ___

Answer: 21   (multiply by 3)

Type 3: Number Logic

Example: If all Blorts are Fings, and some Fings are Zups, which must be true?

A) All Blorts are Zups   B) Some Blorts are Zups   C) No Blorts are Zups   D) Cannot tell

Answer: D — Cannot tell (we know Blorts are Fings, but only some Fings are Zups — we cannot determine if any Blorts are in that subset)

Battery 3: Non-Verbal Reasoning — Sample Question Types

Non-verbal reasoning uses shapes and figures rather than words or numbers. It tests visual logic — the ability to spot patterns, categories and relationships in abstract diagrams.

Type 1: Figure Classification

Three figures share a common property; choose a fourth that belongs in the same group.

Example: Three shapes are shown: a square with a dot inside, a triangle with a dot inside, and a circle with a dot inside. Which of the following belongs in the group?

Options: A) A hexagon with a cross inside   B) A pentagon with a dot inside   C) A square with a line through it   D) An empty circle

Answer: B — A pentagon with a dot inside (all group members have a dot inside any closed shape)

Type 2: Matrices

A 2×2 or 3×3 grid of figures with one cell missing; choose the figure that completes the pattern.

Example concept: Row 1: [Large dark square] [Large light circle] [Large dark triangle]
Row 2: [Small dark square] [Small light circle] [Small dark triangle]
Row 3: [Large light square] [Large dark circle] [?]

Answer: Large light triangle (size and shading follow the pattern of rows 1 and 2)

Battery 4: Spatial Ability — Sample Question Types

Spatial ability tests the ability to mentally manipulate 2D and 3D shapes. This is the battery where children with strong visual-spatial intelligence (common in those who enjoy building, drawing or coding) often excel regardless of academic results.

Type 1: Figure Rotation

A shape is shown at one orientation; choose which answer option shows the same shape rotated (not reflected).

Tip: The key distinction is between a rotation (the shape is turned) and a reflection (the shape is mirrored). A rotated shape and its mirror image are different. Children often confuse these under time pressure. Practice distinguishing rotation from reflection before test day.

Type 2: Shape Folding

A 2D net (unfolded shape) is shown; choose which 3D shape it would make when folded.

Tip: Work through common 3D shapes — cube nets, cuboid nets, pyramid nets — with your child using paper before test day. Physically folding paper nets is the most effective preparation for this question type.

How to Prepare Effectively

The goal of CAT4 preparation is familiarisation, not cramming. The test measures reasoning potential — drilling answers does not increase underlying ability, but practice reduces the novelty effect that depresses scores in children who have never seen these question types.

Evidence-based preparation:

  • Start early: 8–12 weeks of 2–3 sessions per week is more effective than intensive drilling in the final two weeks
  • Use official or high-quality practice materials: GL Assessment (the CAT4 publisher) sells practice materials; Bond 11+ Non-Verbal and Verbal Reasoning books closely mirror the format
  • Spatial ability: Physical manipulation (paper folding, origami, 3D puzzles, Lego architecture) is the best preparation for spatial battery questions
  • Verbal reasoning: Regular independent reading in English is the most sustained way to build vocabulary and verbal logic over time
  • Simulate test conditions: Timed practice under exam conditions (quiet space, no interruptions, strict time limit) is essential before test day
  • Manage anxiety: The CAT4 is designed so most children do not finish. Tell your child: skipping a hard question and moving on is the right strategy

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you prepare for the CAT4 test?

Yes — through familiarisation with the format, not cramming. The CAT4 measures reasoning potential, so drilling answers is not effective. What works: practising the question types, completing timed mock tests, and reducing test anxiety through familiarity with the format.

What CAT4 score do Dubai schools need for Year 7?

KHDA Outstanding schools typically look for SAS 108–120+ depending on selectivity. Jumeirah College is most selective (115–125+); Safa Community School is least selective (100–112+). Schools assess the full application, not the CAT4 score alone.

How long is the CAT4 test?

The full CAT4 (Level D) takes approximately 50–70 minutes including instructions. Each battery is strictly timed. Children are not expected to complete every question — this is by design.

What are the 4 CAT4 batteries?

Verbal Reasoning (language-based logic), Quantitative Reasoning (number patterns and relationships), Non-Verbal Reasoning (figure patterns and matrices), and Spatial Ability (2D/3D shape manipulation).

How is the CAT4 scored?

Results are reported as Standard Age Scores (SAS): mean 100, standard deviation 15. SAS 115+ is well above average. Scores are age-standardised against a large reference population of same-age children.
