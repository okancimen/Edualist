30+ Real International School Interview Questions 2026: Child & Parent

Quick Answer — What Gets Asked in Interviews? Children: "Tell me about yourself", "What's your favourite subject?", "How does it feel to be somewhere new?" Parents: "How would you describe your child's learning style?", "Why did you choose this school?" Dubai and UAE interviews are placement-focused, not elimination-focused — honest, natural answers beat rehearsed scripts every time.

We have another guide covering the general interview preparation framework — a 4-phase, 30-day plan including the parent meeting. This article is its companion: the actual questions that get asked. I have accompanied families to dozens of interviews in Dubai and the UAE; the questions below are drawn directly from those experiences. You cannot memorise every possible question, but knowing the most common ones significantly boosts both your child's and your own confidence.

Remember: International school interviews in Dubai and the UAE are placement-focused, not elimination-focused. The school is asking questions to understand which year group and which programme your child fits best — not to reject them. Knowing this difference is itself a major form of preparation.

Primary School (Ages 5–10) — Child Questions

At this age, questions are deliberately kept simple and social. The school is not measuring academic performance — it is observing your child's ability to communicate, express themselves, and demonstrate social maturity.

"Tell me about yourself. What's your name, how old are you, tell me something about your family."
Why it's asked: To see whether the child can speak comfortably and spontaneously. The ideal answer is short, natural and in their own words — even "My name is Elif, I'm 8, I have two brothers" is perfectly sufficient.

"What do you enjoy most outside school?"
Why it's asked: To understand interests and areas of passion — the school may use this to suggest clubs, sports or activities when placing your child. There is no right or wrong answer.

"How does it feel to be somewhere new?"
Why it's asked: To gauge adaptation capacity. A child saying "scary but exciting" or "a bit hard but I'll get used to it" is far more convincing than a rehearsed "very happy."

"Has there ever been a time at school when you found something hard? What did you do?"
Why it's asked: To understand how the child responds to difficulty. Saying "I asked for help / I tried again / I asked my teacher" paints a strong profile.

"What do you do if you disagree with your best friend?"
Why it's asked: To assess conflict resolution style and empathy. A simple answer like "We talk and try to sort it out" meets expectations well.

Middle School (Ages 11–14) — Child Questions

At this age, questions become more analytical and personal. The school is trying to measure how your child thinks, their self-awareness, and their social consciousness.

"If you could change the world for one day, what would you do?"
This question is not random — the school is looking for alignment between its values and how your child sees the world. What matters is not a "correct" answer but a personal, defensible one. "I'd make sure everyone has clean water, because..." is simple but genuine, and it works.

"Which subject feels most like you? Why?"
Self-awareness is being tested, not academic strength. "Science, because it chases the why and how" or "Literature, because I get to see different perspectives" — these are strong, original answers.

"Do you prefer teamwork or working alone? Why?"
There is no wrong answer. The school wants to hear that your child is aware of their preference and can also adapt to the alternative. A nuanced answer like "I can usually do both, but I tend to..." feels more authentic.

"Is there something specific you want to learn at this school?"
Researching the school's programme in advance and naming something specific — "I saw you have a robotics club, I'd really like to try that" — distinguishes your child's application from others.

Secondary School (Ages 15–18) — Child Questions

At this level, the school wants to understand your child's career vision, the motivation behind their curriculum choice, and their global awareness. Questions are deeper.

"Why do you want to do IB / A-Level / AP specifically?"
"Because it's prestigious" or "my parents wanted it" are answers to avoid. A strong answer: "I researched that IB provides a strong foundation for applying to universities outside Turkey; I'm genuinely interested in the independent study components like the Extended Essay." Specific, researched, authentic.

"Pick a global issue and explain what could be done about it."
A favourite at IB schools. The topic itself matters less than whether your child can see an issue from multiple angles. Single-track solutions versus "on one hand... on the other..." framing shows more mature thinking.

"What is your biggest challenge and how do you manage it?"
Although it looks like a weakness question, it measures self-awareness and a growth mindset. "I struggle with stress management but I've recently started..." is far stronger than "everything's fine, no challenges."

"Why did you choose this school and how do you want to contribute here?"
A two-part question confirming both that your child has researched the school and that they want to participate actively. Answering with a concrete plan — "I'd like to join the debate club and..." — is effective.

Özlem Çimen — Coach's note

The best interview preparation is not question-and-answer drilling — it's talking through these questions with your child at the dinner table in a relaxed way. When you ask "What's hardest for you?" or "What do you like and not like about school?" your child is preparing, and you as a parent are understanding their perspective more deeply. That second gain lasts much longer than the interview itself.

Parent Meeting: Questions the School Actually Asks

The parent meeting typically lasts 15–30 minutes, either alongside or immediately after the child's assessment. The school's goal is not to evaluate you — it is to understand your child better and see whether your family's values align with the school's.

"How would you describe your child's learning style?"
Academic strengths and weaknesses are not what's being asked — it's about how they learn. Observations like "visual learner," "learns by doing," or "keeps asking questions until they fully understand" are very useful.

"What challenges did they face at their previous school?"
Be honest. Problems that are concealed tend to surface during the assessment or in the first few weeks. The school needs to know what to look out for — they don't want surprises.

"Why did you choose this school?"
Avoid "great reputation" or "everyone sends their children here." Prepare an answer that highlights a specific feature, programme or approach you researched.

"What is your family's approach to education?"
The school wants to understand how involved and supportive the family is. Answers that show balance — "we help with homework but let our child make their own decisions" — are strong.

"Is there anything else you'd like us to know about your child?"
Don't leave this blank. It's your opportunity to speak — use it to highlight a strength, a growth area, or a specific circumstance about your child.

Most Common Interview Mistakes

Parents steering the child's answers: Intervening in the interview room shows parental anxiety, not the child's confidence. The admissions team always notices.

Presenting everything as perfect: "We had no difficulties, everything was wonderful" does not build trust. Schools prefer a realistic, growth-focused picture.

Name-dropping other schools: Mentioning "we also applied to GEMS Wellington" is not always well-received by admissions teams. Focus: why is this school right for you?

Over-rehearsing your child: Memorised answers are spotted immediately. IB schools in particular are assessing independent thinking — originality, not formula, is what's measured.

Frequently Asked Questions

What kinds of questions are asked in international school interviews?

Questions vary by age: primary-level interviews focus on social and personal questions; middle school adds analytical thinking; secondary school questions assess career vision and global awareness.

What do schools ask at the parent meeting?

Common parent questions: your child's learning style, challenges at the previous school, why you chose this school, and your family's approach to education.

Can a child with limited English attend an international school interview?

Yes — they go through an EAL (English as an Additional Language) assessment instead. Limited English is not a reason for rejection; it informs the support plan the school creates for your child.

How much preparation time is needed for the interview?

2–3 weeks of natural, relaxed conversation for 20–30 minutes a day is sufficient. The goal is not memorisation — it is helping your child feel comfortable expressing themselves.

What should we do if the interview is unsuccessful?

Request feedback from the school, ask to be placed on the waiting list, and look at alternative schools with a good profile match for your child.
