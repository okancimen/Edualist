My Child Has Exam Anxiety: A Guide to Understanding and Managing It

Child exam anxiety My child is afraid of exams Test anxiety in children EduEntry academic assessment

On the morning of the exam your child says their stomach hurts. The day before they studied hard — they knew the material. But now, with the exam paper in front of them, their mind goes blank. The things they explained at home, they can't say in class. When the exam is over, it all comes back. And it repeats every time.

This is the classic picture of exam anxiety. And the most maddening part for parents is this: your child knows it. They genuinely know it. But in the exam setting they can't reach that knowledge.

Exam anxiety is not a weakness. It is a response that switches on when the brain perceives a threat — and that response genuinely blocks performance. But the good news is this: exam anxiety can be managed. And managing it is a skill — it can be learned.

Is Exam Anxiety Normal? Understanding the Threshold

Every child feels some level of tension before an exam. That's normal and even helpful: a certain level of arousal sharpens attention and supports performance. We can call this "exam stress."

Exam anxiety is a state that has crossed that threshold. The difference: stress creates activation, anxiety paralyses. When the anxiety level passes a certain point, the brain enters threat mode — the amygdala takes over and the prefrontal cortex (the centre for logical thinking and memory access) is suppressed. The result: your child knows it, but can't show it on the exam paper.

This distinction matters, because the "a bit of stress is normal" line can lead us to normalise and ignore genuine exam anxiety. If your child cries before every exam, complains of a stomach ache or headache, can't sleep, or says "I wish I didn't have to sit it" — the threshold has been crossed.

Stress creates activation. Anxiety paralyses. Recognising the difference is the start of the right intervention.

3 Different Symptom Groups

Exam anxiety shows up through three different channels. Recognising all of them matters — because each offers a different point of intervention.

1. Physical Symptoms

This is the most visible group. Before or during the exam: stomach ache, nausea, headache, racing heart, trembling hands or sweating. These symptoms are real — they aren't faked. When the brain perceives a threat, the body physically reacts. When your child says "my stomach hurts," rather than dismissing it, understand the source of that physical response.

Physical symptoms are most quickly regulated with breathing techniques. Deep, slow breathing activates the parasympathetic nervous system and takes the body out of threat mode. Five minutes of deliberate breathing before an exam can significantly reduce the response.

2. Thought Symptoms

The second group is anxious thought patterns. Thoughts like "if I fail this exam everything is ruined," "everyone will see I'm stupid," "I won't manage anyway" repeat before and during the exam. These thoughts are automatic — not a deliberate choice by the child.

Thought symptoms are the hardest to manage, because the child has to notice and question them. Asking "is this thought true? Is it really like that?" is the basis of the cognitive reframing technique. This skill can be developed with repetition.

3. Behavioural Symptoms

The third group is the most often missed. Over-studying before an exam, or the exact opposite — being unable to study at all; not wanting to go to school on exam day; building catastrophe scenarios like "I probably got zero" after walking out; immediately starting to worry about the next exam. These behaviours are part of the anxiety — but from the outside they can be misread as "lazy" or "dramatic."

Recognising behavioural symptoms is especially important, because the wrong responses to them — "you're exaggerating," "everyone sits exams" — strengthen the child's feeling of not being understood and increase the anxiety.

The Mistakes Families Make Without Realising

Parents want to reduce their child's anxiety — but some well-intentioned things can do the opposite.

"Stop exaggerating, everyone sits exams": this sentence invalidates what the child is living through. Anxiety is real, and normalising it doesn't make it pass. Instead: "You feel tense before the exam, and that makes sense. Let's look at what we can do together."

Pre-exam performance pressure: sentences like "you really need to study well this time," "I don't know what we'll do if your score is low" — however genuine the worry behind them — multiply the child's anxiety. The pre-exam period is a time to build confidence, not to lower expectations.

Caring about the outcome more than necessary: "This exam is very important," or reacting excessively when the mark comes in, reinforces the message that the exam is a threat. Framing the exam as important but manageable lowers the anxiety threshold.

Ignoring the anxiety: "It'll pass, don't worry" or "you studied, it'll be fine" — these look comforting but don't genuinely address the child's anxiety. Anxiety ignored grows; anxiety recognised shrinks.

A 4-Step Approach: What Families Can Do

There are four practical steps for working with a child who has exam anxiety. These don't replace expert support — but in most cases they make a notable difference.

  • Step 1 — Recognise and name it: talk openly with your child about exam anxiety. "Sometimes before an exam your mind feels like it goes blank, right? That's very common, and let's look at what we can do about it." Calling anxiety by its name takes it out of the realm of mystery and makes it manageable.
  • Step 2 — Teach a breathing technique: the 4-7-8 technique (breathe in for 4, hold for 7, out for 8) or diaphragmatic breathing can be used to reduce the physical anxiety response. Teach and practise it outside exam periods — teaching it in a moment of crisis doesn't work.
  • Step 3 — Improve preparation quality: part of the anxiety comes from genuine gaps in preparation. With an EduEntry academic assessment you can see your child's true position: which topics are missing, which areas are strong. Turning the unknown into the known makes the anxiety concrete and manageable.
  • Step 4 — Reframe the exam: framings like "this exam doesn't measure you, it measures what you've learned" or "an exam isn't an ending, it's a data point" can be shared with the child. This weakens the power of catastrophe scenarios.

Exam Anxiety and Academic Preparation: What's the Link?

Exam anxiety is not always purely psychological. A significant component is genuine academic uncertainty: as long as "do I actually know this topic? What will come up?" stays unanswered, the anxiety grows.

So one key pillar of managing exam anxiety is building academic confidence. That comes from improving the quality of preparation — targeted study, not the number of hours. Which topics are genuinely missing, which ones are weak — preparation done without knowing this does not reduce the uncertainty.

This link is often paired with another: a child who won't study and a child with exam anxiety frequently appear together and feed each other. Avoiding study increases anxiety; rising anxiety makes studying even harder.

Academic coaching breaks this loop by both building the preparation system and supporting the student's anxiety-management skills. As the study plan becomes clear, the uncertainty falls; as uncertainty falls, the anxiety eases.

Exam anxiety should also be considered alongside adolescent mental health as a whole — especially if the anxiety spills beyond school.

What is the difference between exam anxiety and exam stress?

Exam stress is a normal response felt before a test that can actually improve performance — a certain level of stress supports focus and motivation. Exam anxiety has crossed that threshold: because the brain perceives a threat, thinking capacity narrows, the child can't express what they know, physical symptoms appear (stomach ache, nausea, racing heart), and the loop harms learning too. In short, stress creates activation; anxiety paralyses.

Will my child's exam anxiety go away?

Exam anxiety can be managed and reduced with the right approach. Anxiety-management techniques (breathing exercises, gradual exposure, cognitive reframing) work effectively with children. But severity and duration matter: mild anxiety limited to exam periods can ease with family support, while intense, constant anxiety needs professional help. Anxiety isn't "cured" — it's managed. And that management is a skill that is learned.

When does my child's exam anxiety need psychological support?

See a child-and-adolescent psychologist when: the anxiety isn't limited to exams but affects daily life; the child refuses school or physical symptoms become constant; sleep disturbance, loss of appetite or social withdrawal accompany it; or there's no progress in 4-6 weeks despite family and teacher support. Early intervention is far more effective — addressed before it deepens, anxiety is easier to manage.
