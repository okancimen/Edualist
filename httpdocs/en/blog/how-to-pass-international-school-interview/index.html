How to Pass an International School Interview: Guide for Children & Parents

Applying to an international school is not a single exam — it's a process. Written assessment, child interview, parent meeting, sometimes even a supervised playdate. Every stage is the school trying to answer one question: "Are this child and this family right for our community?"

Özlem Çimen — Note from the coach

When we went to our first school interview, we were completely unprepared. My child walked into the room, the door closed — I waited outside, and those five minutes were among the longest of my life. Since then I've prepared hundreds of families for interviews. I know with certainty now: the right preparation genuinely makes a difference. This guide was written from the notes I made while standing outside that door.

What the interview is actually assessing: Academic ability, yes — but the real goal is to understand your child's adaptability, social skills, curiosity, and whether the family's values align with the school's philosophy.

How to Pass an International School Interview

Passing an international school interview comes down to three things: preparation before the day, how your child presents their curiosity and social confidence in the room, and how clearly you as a parent articulate alignment with the school's values. Most families arrive unprepared — that's where the difference is made.

The 4 Stages of the Process

1
Written Assessment (Writing + Maths)
Most international schools require a written assessment for Grade 3 and above. Tests run 45 minutes to 1 hour. Difficulty increases with age. Reading comprehension and maths problem-solving are the core areas.
2
Child Interview (30–45 minutes)
Your child meets a teacher or admissions coordinator alone in a room — you wait elsewhere. The school is assessing adaptability, social confidence and language ability, not academic knowledge.
3
Parent Interview (30–45 minutes)
The Director or admissions staff discusses school philosophy, family expectations and educational goals with you. Most questions are open-ended — there is no single "right" answer, but authenticity is read immediately.
4
School Tour (optional)
Most schools show you the corridors, classrooms and labs after the interview. This also lets the school observe how your child responds to the environment — interested, curious, comfortable.

Common Child Interview Questions

The categories below come up in almost every interview, with example approaches. For full lists of real questions by age group and detailed parent interview questions, see our real international school interview questions guide →

Q: What is your name and how old are you?
State your full name and age clearly. Then add one calm, natural sentence: "I live in Dubai and I'm currently at another school." Don't rush — speak at a comfortable pace.
Q: What activity do you enjoy most?
Be specific and genuine — not "playing games" but "playing football" or "reading." Then explain why: "Because I love running fast" or "because I can imagine a different world." Brief is better than rehearsed.
Q: Tell me about a time school was difficult for you.
Choose a real challenge — and describe how you dealt with it: "At first I found long division hard, but I asked my teacher for help and kept practising until it clicked." Problem + response = maturity.
Q: How do you make new friends?
Show social awareness without pretending to be outgoing if you're not: "I can be a bit shy at first, but once I find something in common with someone I make friends quickly." Honesty reads better than performance.
Q: What do you think about school rules?
Show maturity: "Rules are necessary — they keep everyone safe and help us learn. If I don't understand a rule, I ask my teacher rather than just ignoring it."

Preparing for the Parent Interview

✓ Right Approaches
  • Explain why you chose this specific school — authentically
  • Highlight your child's distinctive qualities
  • Articulate your family's educational philosophy clearly
  • Describe how you support learning at home with concrete examples
  • Demonstrate that you've researched the school — reference something specific
✗ What to Avoid
  • Giving the impression you chose the school purely for prestige
  • Presenting the school as a back-up plan
  • Over-focusing on your child's weaknesses
  • Openly disagreeing with school policies in the interview
  • Appearing to have done no research on the school at all

30-Day Preparation Plan

Weeks 1–2: Foundation Preparation
Study the school's website and social media in detail. Have curious, genuine conversations with your child about the school — not briefings. Assess your child's English level: will the interview be conducted in English? If yes, start building vocabulary and comfort now.
Week 3: Written Assessment Practice
Work through practice papers. Practise reading comprehension passages. Focus maths revision on weaker areas (fractions, geometry, word problems). Always practise under timed conditions. To measure your child's academic level objectively before the interview, consider the EduEntry international academic assessment →
Week 4: Mock Interview
Conduct a mock interview at home — you as the interviewer, your child as the student. Start with easy questions, gradually increase difficulty. Practise eye contact and body language together. The goal is comfort, not a rehearsed script.

Interview Day Checklist

Leave early — being late is the one thing you can completely control.
Ensure your child is dressed neatly and feels comfortable in what they're wearing.
Keep your own energy calm and positive — children feel parental anxiety directly.
Note the interviewer's name and position when you arrive.
If you have questions, ask them at the end — don't interrupt the flow.
After leaving, don't immediately ask "how did it go?" — give your child space to decompress first.

After the Interview

Schools typically notify families within 2–4 weeks. During that period, continue evaluating other schools — do not put everything on one outcome. If you receive an offer, notify other schools within the deadline they've given you. If you receive a rejection, ask for feedback — it's often given and always useful.

From Özlem: "Passing" the interview and "being the right fit for that school" are not the same thing. Which school aligns with your family's values? That question should come before everything else.

Common Mistakes Families Make

The most common trap is trying to teach your child a single "correct" answer. Experienced interviewers recognise rehearsed responses immediately — authenticity is always stronger and more convincing.

The second common mistake is not preparing adequately for the parent interview. Families who appear to have done no research on the school create a negative impression about how seriously they are taking the application.

Finally: going to an interview hungry or tired — for both child and parent — measurably reduces performance. Plan a relaxed breakfast the morning of the interview and leave early enough to arrive calm.
