My Child Is Starting an International School: 5 Critical Mistakes to Avoid

child starting international school international school transition international school preparation international school adaptation

Orientation day. The school has gathered all the parents. The deputy head steps up and opens with: "Here, the learning process matters more than grades." The father sitting next to me leans over and whispers: "What does that mean?" I don't know either. We spent two years on English courses, CAT4 preparation and the admissions process. And now the woman at the front was talking about something entirely different — and neither of us understood it.

That day, I understood the gap: getting into an international school and succeeding in one are entirely different things. And most families, unfortunately, discover this difference somewhere in the middle of the first year.

I have worked closely with international school transitions at Edualist for years — families from Turkey, the Gulf, Europe. The same mistakes appear again and again. Here are the five — and what to do instead.

Mistake 1: Seeing the Transition as a Logistics Problem Rather Than a Skills Problem

Most families focus on: "Is their English good enough?" This is a necessary question, but not a sufficient one. The real question is: can my child work independently?

In most national education systems, students are guided heavily. What to study, when to study, how to study — the teacher sets the structure. In international systems — especially IB and A-Level — the expectation is different. Students build their own study schedule, run multiple projects in parallel and do independent research without being told to.

This is not an academic skill. It is a management skill. And most families move without ever asking whether their child has it.

What to do: Before the transition, run a few weeks of "independent project" with your child. Choose a topic, set a deadline, ask them to complete it without your involvement. Watch: do they plan? Do they procrastinate? Do they submit without being reminded? This observation will tell you more about how much support the first year requires than any admissions result.

Getting into an international school is one skill set. Succeeding in one is an entirely different skill set.

Mistake 2: Assuming the English Level Is Sufficient

They scored 6.5 on IELTS, passed the admissions test, the school accepted them. So their English must be good enough. This logic is sound — but in practice it is often misleading.

Entry tests measure everyday communicative English. But success in an international school requires something much more specific: argument construction, counter-argument analysis, source citation, oral debate. These are all separate, learnable academic English skills.

Most international schools have EAL (English as an Additional Language) support. But most families don't know it exists — or don't know how to request it. Before school starts, speaking with the EAL coordinator to understand what language support your child can access is one of the most valuable things you can do.

Practical step: Ask directly: "Can my child access additional support for academic writing?" Asking this question does not signal weakness — it signals preparation.

Mistake 3: Not Measuring Academic Level Before the Transition

Your child was successful in their previous school. They were in the top half of the class, scoring above average. This does not show where they stand on an international benchmark.

National curricula and international curricula differ in meaningful ways. Some areas where national systems are strong; others where they leave gaps. Strong mathematical procedures but weak analytical problem-solving. Solid memorisation but underdeveloped explanation skills. Arriving at an international school without knowing this creates unpleasant surprises in the first year.

An objective assessment — such as EduEntry or the CAT4 test — shows exactly where a child stands against international standards: strong areas and specific gaps. Running this assessment before the transition is one of the most effective ways to minimise first-year surprises.

Mistake 4: Carrying the Previous School's Definition of "Success" Into the New One

This is perhaps the most subtle and most damaging mistake.

In most national systems, success is defined by: high marks, class ranking, exam performance. The child has internalised this. The parents have too. When they carry that definition into an international school, they hit a serious problem: IB and A-Level scores often look very low by national standards.

The IB uses a 7-point scale. Most students score 5–6 — which is a good result. A student used to scoring 85–90 in a national system may feel like a failure at 5 — even though the context is entirely different.

Beyond this, the peer group in an international school is different. A child who was "top of the class" at home may now be in a significantly more competitive environment. Continuing to make that comparison — explicitly or implicitly — is damaging for both child and family.

What to do: Have an open conversation before the first term: "Here, grades mean something different. What matters is what you learn and how you think." This conversation, had before school starts rather than after the first report, significantly softens the grade-shock of term one.

Mistake 5: Leaving the First Year Unsupported

Families often think: "They're in school now — the school will handle it." But in international schools, the first year — especially the very first — is a multi-dimensional challenge, and the child may need substantial support to navigate it.

Academic dimension: Curriculum differences, new study methods, independent project management.
Social dimension: Building relationships across cultures, reading social dynamics, working through the language barrier.
Identity dimension: "Who am I here?" — especially for adolescents, this is a very real challenge.

All three arriving simultaneously makes the first year exceptionally hard. And families usually notice this when the child is already exhausted and sometimes starting to give up.

Academic coaching provides not just academic but holistic support through this period. Structured weekly sessions, regular parent-coach communication and EAL resources together significantly reduce the first-year strain.

First-Term Checklist: The First 3 Months

  • 2–3 months before the transition: Run an academic assessment (EduEntry or CAT4) to identify where your child stands against international standards.
  • 1–2 months before: Contact the school's EAL coordinator; understand what language support is available.
  • Before school starts: Have a first session with an academic coach — map the new system's expectations and build a plan for the first month.
  • First month: Evaluate by "what did they learn?" not by "what did they score?" Avoid creating grade pressure in month one.
  • First 3 months: A weekly family check-in — not academic, emotional: "How are you feeling? What's hard? What do you enjoy?"
  • End of first term: Review the school report with the academic coach; update the plan for term two.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is a child ready for an international school?

Readiness is not just about English level. Independent study skills, self-management and a sufficiently strong academic foundation in the target curriculum are equally important. An objective assessment — such as EduEntry or CAT4 — can show where a child stands against international benchmarks before the transition, so surprises in the first year are minimised.

Is the transition hard even if a child's English is strong?

Yes. Conversational English and academic English are very different. IB and A-Level require analytical writing, source evaluation and structured class discussion — skills that go well beyond what language tests typically measure. Study habits, project management and independent research skills also determine how difficult the transition is.

When should academic coaching start for a school transition?

Ideally 2–3 months before school starts. That window allows time to assess the child's actual academic level, close specific gaps and build familiarity with what the new system expects. Continuing coaching through the first term significantly reduces the adjustment pain.
