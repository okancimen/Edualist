"His English Was Good" — The Dubai International School Reality

BICS vs CALP Academic English Dubai international school Child English gap international school EAL programme Dubai

I'm writing this as both an education consultant and a parent. When my son started at an international school in Dubai, I had spent 20 years working in international schools. I still got this wrong.

His English was good. That's what his teacher said. That's what the report cards said. That was the consensus of everyone who had seen him in a classroom in Turkey. He had been learning English since age six. He was 11 when we moved to Dubai.

At his new school, the head of English pulled me aside after the first week. She was kind about it. But the message was clear: based on the assessment they had done, his English was approximately three years behind where it needed to be to access the academic curriculum independently.

I am an international education professional. And I still had not understood the difference between the English my son had and the English the school needed.

Two Kinds of English: BICS and CALP

The distinction that explains everything is one that most families — and many teachers — never encounter until they're inside the international school system.

Type What it covers Development time How it looks
BICS Social conversation, instructions, playground, cafeteria 6–12 months Child chatting fluently with peers
CALP Academic writing, subject reading, abstract arguments 3–7 years Strong academic work, independent reading

BICS (Basic Interpersonal Communication Skills) is the English you use to navigate social life. Within 6–12 months of immersion, most children develop strong BICS — it's context-supported, body-language-rich, and forgiving of imprecision. My son had good BICS. He could watch English films, play online games in English, have basic conversations.

CALP (Cognitive Academic Language Proficiency) is the English the school tests you on. Writing an essay on the causes of World War I. Reading and analysing a scientific text. Understanding abstract grammar instruction. Constructing a formal argument in writing. These require deep familiarity with academic vocabulary and structures that are genuinely different from social English — and they take years to develop.

"His conversational English is fine. But in terms of the academic language he needs to access the curriculum independently — he needs significant support. He's approximately in Year 5 academically in English, not Year 8." — Head of English, Dubai international school

I had been so focused on whether he could communicate in English that I had not measured whether he could study in English. These are not the same thing.

Why Turkish-School English Doesn't Transfer

Turkish schools are not bad at teaching English. The problem is structural: English in a national school system is taught as a subject, in Turkish, with the goal of passing national examinations. English in an international school is the medium of instruction for every subject. These produce fundamentally different language profiles.

Child from Turkish national school Child already at international school
Grammar knowledge Often strong Variable
Academic vocabulary Limited Strong
Essay writing in English Not practised Years of practice
Subject reading in English Not practised Daily
BICS Moderate Strong
CALP Weak Strong

The gap is not about intelligence. It is about exposure time. A child who has been learning in English for three years has three years' worth of academic vocabulary, writing practice, and reading volume. A child arriving from a national system is not behind in intelligence — they are behind in accumulated academic language practice. And that gap has to be explicitly closed.

The gap between Turkish-school English and international school English is real, measurable, and closable. The mistake is not knowing it exists until the school tells you on day one.

What Happened Next

My son entered the EAL programme. It was effective and well-run — the school knew what they were doing. Within 18 months, his academic English had caught up sufficiently to exit EAL support. He went on to perform well academically.

But those 18 months were harder than they needed to be. With 6 months of targeted academic English preparation before the move — specifically CALP, not BICS — the gap would have been smaller and the catching-up less disruptive. I know this now because I have guided dozens of families through exactly this process, and the ones who prepare specifically for academic English arrive at school with a meaningfully smaller gap.

The question isn't why your child can't learn English — it's whether the English they have is the right kind for where they're going.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between BICS and CALP?

BICS (Basic Interpersonal Communication Skills) is the English used in everyday social situations — conversations with peers, understanding instructions, navigating the cafeteria. It develops within 6–12 months of immersion. CALP (Cognitive Academic Language Proficiency) is the English needed for academic work — writing essays, reading subject-specific texts, understanding academic vocabulary, constructing formal arguments. It takes 3–7 years to develop fully. A child can appear fluent in BICS and still be significantly behind in CALP — which directly determines academic grades.

Why does good conversational English not mean good academic English?

Conversational English (BICS) and academic English (CALP) draw on different language systems. Social English relies on context, body language, shared reference, and real-time interaction — all of which support understanding even with limited vocabulary. Academic English requires processing abstract content without these supports, using subject-specific vocabulary, producing extended written arguments, and understanding dense written texts. These skills require years of explicit exposure and instruction. Many children who appear fluent in social English are genuinely behind in academic English — it's not a performance issue, it's a language development issue.

What is an EAL programme and how long does it take?

EAL (English as Additional Language) is in-school support for students whose first language is not English. In Dubai international schools, EAL programmes typically involve additional English instruction, modified assessments in some subjects, and regular progress monitoring. Duration varies significantly by school and by student: some students exit EAL support within one academic year; others receive support for two to three years. EAL is effective support — but it means the student is on a different academic track from peers, and it's worth trying to build English as far as possible before school starts to minimise the EAL period.

How do I know if my child's English is strong enough for a Dubai international school?

The clearest signal is an objective CEFR assessment. For Dubai international schools, B1 is the practical threshold for joining academic classes without EAL support. Below A2, EAL support is almost certain. But CEFR level alone is not enough — the type of English matters. A child can test at B1 overall while having strong BICS and weak CALP. The EduEntry assessment specifically evaluates academic language skills alongside overall proficiency, which gives a more accurate picture of international school readiness.

My child's English teacher said they had "good English" — why were they placed in EAL?

Teachers in home-country schools typically assess English relative to national standards — which are very different from the standard expected at a fully English-medium international school. A student who is in the top English group at a Turkish national school may have strong BICS (social English) but limited CALP (academic English) compared to peers at an IB or British school who have been using English as their primary academic language for years. The gap is real — it's not a reflection of ability.
